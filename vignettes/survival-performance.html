Computation time of survival fits

Multilevel unanchored meta-regression (ML-UMR) compares a treatment for which individual patient data are available from a single-arm study with a comparator for which only aggregate evidence is published, adjusting for differences between the covariate distributions of the two populations (Chandler and Ishak 2026a). In its extension to time-to-event outcomes (Chandler and Ishak 2026b), the likelihood of the comparator evidence is integrated over the covariate distribution of the comparator population, which multiplies the number of likelihood terms by the number of integration points. The cost of evaluating the log posterior density, and hence the duration of a fit, is therefore dominated by the comparator likelihood.

The mlumr() argument optimization controls computational optimizations: changes to the way in which the log posterior density and its gradient are evaluated that leave the statistical model, and therefore the posterior distribution, unchanged. This article documents the optimization introduced in version 0.2.1, the per-arm reuse of the comparator linear predictor in the survival models (hereafter predictor reuse), and reports a simulation study of its effect on computation time. Optimizations introduced in later releases will be documented and evaluated in this article in the same manner.

The optimization argument

Optimizations are enabled by default and are disabled with optimization = FALSE:

fit <- mlumr(dat, distribution = "weibull")                          # predictor reuse (default)
fit_ref <- mlumr(dat, distribution = "weibull", optimization = FALSE) # as in releases before 0.2.1

The setting is stored in the fitted object as fit$model_controls$optimization, and prior_sensitivity() refits the model with the same setting. In the present release the argument affects the survival family only; it has no effect for binary, continuous, or count outcomes. It is unrelated to Stan’s optimization algorithms: the posterior distribution is always explored by Markov chain Monte Carlo sampling.

Disabling optimizations serves two purposes: benchmarking, as in the study reported below, and reproducing, operation by operation, a fit obtained with an earlier release.

The modified computation

In a survival fit the comparator evidence consists of reconstructed pseudo-individual data, and the likelihood of each pseudo-individual is integrated over the covariate distribution of the comparator population, in the manner of the population adjustment of multilevel network meta-regression (Phillippo et al. 2020). With integration points \(x_{a1}, \dots, x_{aM}\) for comparator arm \(a\), the likelihood contribution of pseudo-individual \(j\), with observed time \(t_j\) and event indicator \(d_j\), is

\[ L_j = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^{M} f\!\left(t_j, d_j \mid \eta_{am}\right), \qquad \eta_{am} = \mu_{C} + x_{am}^{\top} \beta, \]

where \(f\) is the density or survival function of the chosen distribution, \(\mu_C\) is the comparator intercept, and \(\beta\) is the vector of comparator covariate coefficients. The linear predictors \(\eta_{a1}, \dots, \eta_{aM}\) depend on the arm and on the parameters but not on \(j\). Releases before 0.2.1 nevertheless recomputed them for every pseudo-individual: one product of the \(M \times n_B\) design matrix with the coefficient vector per pseudo-individual and per evaluation of the log density, where \(n_B\) is the number of design columns. With predictor reuse they are computed once per arm and evaluation and shared by all pseudo-individuals in the arm.

Stan evaluates the gradient of the log density by reverse-mode automatic differentiation (Carpenter et al. 2017), and the No-U-Turn sampler (Hoffman and Gelman 2014) evaluates this gradient at every leapfrog step. Each matrix-vector product adds \(M\) nodes to the expression graph and, in the reverse pass, propagates \(M\) adjoints into \(n_B\) coefficients. Predictor reuse removes all but one product per arm. It leaves unchanged the \(n_{\text{agd}} \times M\) likelihood terms \(f\), the likelihood of the index study, and the priors. The relative reduction per evaluation therefore equals the share of the total cost that is attributable to the removed products. It is expected to be larger when the design is wider (more covariates, or the relaxed model with its separate comparator coefficients) and the likelihood terms are inexpensive, and smaller when the likelihood terms or the index study dominate the cost.

Both computations evaluate the same function. The matrix-vector products yield identical values, but the reverse pass accumulates the contributions to each coefficient’s gradient in a different order: once per arm after summation over pseudo-individuals, rather than once per pseudo-individual. Because floating-point addition is not associative, the two gradients may differ in their final digits. The posterior distribution is identical; under a common seed the draws coincide until such differences accumulate along the sampler’s trajectory, and they agree in distribution throughout. The package’s parity tests (tests/parity/ in the source repository) compare, for each of 52 workloads and both settings, the log density, its gradient, the parameter transforms, and every generated quantity against the 0.2.0 models.

Simulation study design

The design is reported following the ADEMP structure of Morris et al. (2019): aims, data-generating mechanisms, estimands, methods, and performance measures. The estimands are computational rather than statistical: the study measures computation time and verifies numerical agreement, rather than bias or coverage.

The study was implemented in the script data-raw/simulate_survival_performance.R, which is part of the package source repository but is not installed with the package. The script writes, alongside its results, a manifest that records the software and hardware used. The results reported below were computed from files with MD5 digests cdf8546e6f7a093f725259a8dac61d22 (per-evaluation timings) and 654dc369f68c9d2007a96f62d21dfa81 (complete fits); a package test verifies these digests against the files in the repository.

Aims

  1. A1. To estimate the reduction in time per evaluation of the log density and its gradient, the operation repeated at every leapfrog step of the sampler, and its dependence on the fitted distribution, the model, the number of comparator pseudo-individuals \(n_{\text{agd}}\), the number of integration points \(M\), and the number of covariates \(K\).
  2. A2. To estimate the part of that reduction that carries over to a complete mlumr() fit.
  3. A3. To verify that the computations with and without predictor reuse, and the 0.2.0 models, produced the same values in every run of the study.

Data-generating mechanism

A single data-generating mechanism was used throughout, because the computation time depends on the code path of the fitted model and, for given sample sizes, depends on the data only through the mix of event and censoring indicators.

  • An index study of 200 individuals and a comparator cohort of \(n_{\text{agd}}\) individuals were generated in the same way.
  • The \(K\) covariates alternated between continuous (normal, SD 1) and binary. The comparator population was shifted by 0.2 on the continuous covariates and had prevalence 0.45, against 0.5 in the index population, on the binary covariates.
  • Event times followed a Weibull proportional hazards model with shape 1.2, coefficients 0.3, -0.2, 0.15, -0.1, 0.25, and -0.15 (the first \(K\) of these), a baseline median of approximately five months, and a conditional log hazard ratio of -0.4 for the index treatment.
  • Censoring times were independent and exponentially distributed with rate 0.02 per month, with administrative censoring at 24 months: right censoring only, without delayed entry.
  • The comparator cohort’s times and event indicators were supplied to set_agd_surv() as its pseudo-individual data, together with its realized covariate means, standard deviations, and proportions. For the purposes of computation this is equivalent to a digitized Kaplan-Meier curve.
  • Integration points were generated by add_integration() with its default Gaussian copula. The restricted mean survival time (RMST) was computed to 18 months, and predictions were made at 6, 12, and 18 months.

Each data set was shared by every fitted distribution and model of the same size, so that these comparisons were made on the same individuals.

Methods compared

  • With predictor reuse (optimization = TRUE): the 0.2.1 survival models, which compute the linear predictor once per comparator arm.
  • Without predictor reuse (optimization = FALSE): the same compiled models, which recompute the linear predictor for every pseudo-individual.
  • Version 0.2.0: the survival models as released in mlumr 0.2.0, retained unchanged in the repository for the parity tests. This comparison addresses two further questions: whether version 0.2.1 is faster than the previous release, and whether the switch between the two computations adds any cost when predictor reuse is disabled.

Level A: time per evaluation

Workloads. The workloads comprised every combination of fitted distribution (Weibull, log-normal, generalized gamma, and the M-spline flexible baseline), model (SPFA and relaxed), \(n_{\text{agd}}\) (100, 500, 2000), \(M\) (16, 64, 256), and \(K\) (1, 3, 6): 216 workloads, each with two data sets. The piecewise exponential baseline shares the likelihood code of the M-spline baseline and was not measured separately.

Measurement. Time was measured inside the CmdStan executable used by mlumr(engine = "cmdstanr"), through CmdStan’s log_prob method, which evaluates the log density and its gradient at every row of a file of parameter values. The difference between the CPU time of a run over \(N\) rows and that of a run over five rows, divided by \(N - 5\), estimates the cost of a single evaluation with process start-up, data input, and model construction removed. \(N\) was chosen for each data set so that a run lasted approximately 0.5 s, and was the same for all three methods.

Parameter values. The parameter values were five draws from CmdStan’s Laplace approximation at the posterior mode of each data set, so that timings were taken in the region of the parameter space explored by the sampler. For 67 of the 432 data sets (57 generalized gamma and 10 Weibull) the posterior mode could not be located, and the origin together with four small perturbations of it was used instead.

Pairing. Each data set was timed in eight blocks. Within a block, each of the three methods was run once, in an order balanced so that each method occupied the first, second, and third position equally often across blocks. Data sets were processed in random order, so that any temporal drift in timing was distributed across the experimental factors.

Estimand and uncertainty. For each block, the estimand was the log ratio of the time per evaluation without predictor reuse to the time with it, and likewise for the 0.2.0 models. The estimate for a workload was the mean of its 16 block log ratios (two data sets of eight blocks each), with a 95% interval from the \(t\) distribution. Results are reported as the percentage reduction in time per evaluation, \(100\,(1 - 1/\text{ratio})\).

Level B: complete fits

Scenarios. Two SPFA scenarios with three covariates and 64 integration points were studied: a Weibull baseline with 500 comparator pseudo-individuals (ten data sets) and an M-spline baseline with 300 (five data sets). The smaller number of M-spline data sets, which reflects the longer run time of these fits, was fixed before any fit was run.

Fits. Each data set was fitted with optimization = TRUE and with optimization = FALSE through mlumr(engine = "cmdstanr") with the default sampler settings (four chains of 2000 iterations, of which 1000 were warmup, adapt_delta = 0.95, max_treedepth = 15). The four chains ran in parallel, and both fits used the same seed and hence the same initial values. The order of the two settings alternated between data sets. Draws were written with 18 significant digits, so that a chain could be recognized as following a common trajectory under both settings: its draws agreed to a relative tolerance of \(10^{-6}\) at every sampling iteration, a tolerance that admits the rounding differences described above but not a separation of trajectories.

Estimands. The primary estimand was the sampling time per leapfrog step, paired by data set and chain. It measures the cost of a step within the actual sampler and does not depend on the number of steps that a fit happened to take. The secondary estimands were the elapsed time of the mlumr() call and the bulk effective sample size (ESS) of the comparator-population RMST difference per second of elapsed time. Each was summarized as the mean of the log ratios over data sets, with a \(t\) interval; with five or ten data sets these intervals are necessarily wide.

Discussion

Principal findings

Time per evaluation (A1). Averaged over the other factors, predictor reuse reduced the time per evaluation of the log density and its gradient for every distribution and for both models. The 95% interval excluded no change in 196 of the 216 workloads, and in none did it indicate an increase. The reduction was largest for the M-spline baseline (19.4% on average over the other factors) and smallest for the generalized gamma distribution (5.2%), whose likelihood terms evaluate incomplete gamma functions and therefore leave the removed products a smaller share of the total cost.

The dependence on the other factors agreed with the structure of the computation. The reduction increased with the number of covariates, from 11.3% with one covariate to 14.3% with six, and was larger for the relaxed model than for the SPFA model (13.4% against 12.2%), since both widen the design. It increased slightly with the number of pseudo-individuals, from 11.4% at 100 to 13.6% at 2000, as the fixed cost of the index study’s likelihood became a smaller share of the total. It decreased with the number of integration points, from 16.2% at 16 to 10.8% at 256. The operation count does not predict this dependence, because both the removed products and the likelihood terms grow linearly with \(M\); it is consistent with a cost per product that does not grow with \(M\), such as the creation and traversal of the product’s node in the expression graph.

The reductions relative to the 0.2.0 models were close to those relative to the computation without predictor reuse, because the latter took essentially the same time as the 0.2.0 models: over all workloads, the geometric mean ratio of the time of the 0.2.0 models to that without predictor reuse was 1.005. The data flag that selects between the two computations therefore adds no measurable cost.

Complete fits (A2). In the Weibull scenario predictor reuse reduced the sampling time per leapfrog step by 8.6% (95% interval 2.2% to 14.7%), in agreement with the Level A estimate of 10.1% (95% interval 7.2% to 12.9%) for the same distribution, model, sample size, number of integration points, and number of covariates. The elapsed time of the mlumr() call was reduced by 7.6% (95% interval 0.5% to 14.1%), and the bulk ESS of the RMST difference per second increased by a factor of 1.08 (95% interval 1.00 to 1.17). In the M-spline scenario the point estimates were larger, a reduction of 17.2% (95% interval -11.0% to 38.3%) per leapfrog step and 27.0% (95% interval -2.9% to 48.2%) in elapsed time, compared with 14.9% (95% interval 9.8% to 19.7%) per evaluation in Level A at 500 pseudo-individuals. With five data sets these intervals include no change, and the M-spline estimates are not conclusive.

Agreement (A3). At every parameter value timed, the log density and its gradient computed with and without predictor reuse, and by the 0.2.0 models, agreed to within 1.6e-10 in absolute value. In the complete fits, 31 of the 40 Weibull chains and 0 of the 20 M-spline chains followed a common trajectory under both settings throughout sampling. The other chains separated, as expected once differences in the final digits of the gradient accumulate along a trajectory. In every pair of fits the posterior means of the RMST difference differed by at most 0.027 posterior standard deviations, or 1.4 times the Monte Carlo standard error of their difference, which is consistent with Monte Carlo variation alone.

Limitations

All timings were obtained on one machine with one compiler, and through CmdStan (engine = "cmdstanr") only. Absolute times, and to a lesser extent the relative reductions, depend on the processor, the compiler, and its optimization flags; the results are not claimed to hold for other hardware or for the rstan engine.

The two levels measure different quantities. Level A reports the CPU time of a single process that evaluates the log density and its gradient at fixed parameter values. Level B reports the elapsed time of complete mlumr() calls whose four chains ran in parallel. Besides the gradient evaluations of warmup and sampling, this time includes data preparation, the generated quantities (computed once per draw and without automatic differentiation), reading the sampler output, and post-processing. Predictor reuse affects these components little or not at all, so the Level A reductions are an approximate upper limit for the reduction in elapsed time.

The interval for each Level A workload pools 16 block log ratios from two data sets as if they were exchangeable. The time ratio is expected to vary little between data sets of the same size, because it is determined by the code path rather than by the data values, but the interval does not separate variation between data sets from variation within them.

Level B comprised two scenarios with ten and five data sets, and its intervals are correspondingly wide. The reduction in elapsed time for other distributions, sample sizes, numbers of integration points, or for the relaxed model was not measured directly and can be inferred only from the corresponding Level A workloads.

A single data-generating mechanism was used, with right censoring only. Delayed entry and left or interval censoring add terms to each likelihood evaluation and would be expected to reduce the share of the total cost that predictor reuse removes. For 67 data sets the timings were taken at fallback parameter values rather than at draws near the posterior mode. The cost of the incomplete gamma functions in the generalized gamma likelihood depends on the parameter values, but the reductions at the two kinds of points were nearly identical (see Results).

Although each unit started only when the load average was below the stated threshold, the machine was not otherwise idle; the recorded load averages are reported above. Background activity adds variability to individual timings, while the randomized order of data sets and the balanced order of methods within blocks prevent it from favoring either method systematically. In Level B, the second fit of each pair started under the residual load of the first. The alternation of the two settings balanced this exactly for the Weibull scenario (5 of 10 data sets with predictor reuse first) and approximately for the M-spline scenario (3 of 5). Weighting the two orders equally gives reductions per leapfrog step of 8.6% for the Weibull scenario and 18.5% for the M-spline scenario.

Finally, the processor has four performance cores and four efficiency cores, and the assignment of processes to core types was left to the operating system. A chain scheduled on an efficiency core runs more slowly, which is a plausible source of the variability between data sets in Level B, where four chains ran concurrently.

Conclusion

Predictor reuse removes redundant computation from the survival models without changing the model, the posterior distribution, or the computed values beyond the final digits of floating-point sums. In this study it reduced the time per evaluation of the log density and its gradient by 12.4% in the median workload and by up to 30%, with the largest reductions for the M-spline baseline, wide designs, and few integration points, and the smallest for the generalized gamma distribution. In complete Weibull fits the reduction in elapsed time was of the order of 8%, smaller than the reduction per evaluation, as expected from the parts of a fit that predictor reuse does not affect. Because the optimization is enabled by default, these reductions apply to survival fits without any change to user code; optimization = FALSE remains available for benchmarking and for reproducing fits made with earlier releases.

Reproducibility

The study can be reproduced from the root of the package source, with that source installed:

# Each stage records every completed unit and resumes from there if interrupted.
system("Rscript data-raw/simulate_survival_performance.R fixed")
system("Rscript data-raw/simulate_survival_performance.R fits")
system("Rscript data-raw/simulate_survival_performance.R assemble")

The final stage writes the results files and the manifest, which records the commit of the package source, the software versions, and the machine.
