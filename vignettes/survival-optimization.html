Survival fits with and without optimization

Survival fits spend most of their time on the comparator likelihood. Each reconstructed comparator pseudo-individual is integrated over the covariate distribution of the comparator population (Chandler and Ishak 2026), so the comparator contributes one likelihood term per pseudo-individual and integration point, and the sampler evaluates all of them, with their gradient, at every leapfrog step.

From version 0.2.1 the survival models compute the comparator linear predictor once per arm instead of once per pseudo-individual. This predictor reuse is on by default. It changes how the log density is computed, not the model and not the posterior distribution.

This vignette covers three things:

  1. how to turn the optimization off, and when that is useful;
  2. what predictor reuse changes in the computation, and why draws can still differ in their final digits;
  3. a simulation study that compares survival fits with and without the optimization, both per evaluation of the log density and in complete fits.

The optimization argument

library(mlumr)

# Predictor reuse (the default).
fit <- mlumr(dat, distribution = "weibull")
# The computation of releases before 0.2.1.
fit_ref <- mlumr(dat, distribution = "weibull", optimization = FALSE)

The setting is stored in the fit as fit$model_controls$optimization, and prior_sensitivity() refits the model with the same setting. A fit made with a release before 0.2.1 records no setting; prior_sensitivity() refits it with optimization = FALSE, the computation it used.

In this release the argument affects survival fits only. Binary, continuous, and count fits ignore it. It is unrelated to Stan’s optimization algorithms: the posterior distribution is always explored by Markov chain Monte Carlo sampling.

There are two reasons to turn the optimization off:

  • to benchmark it, as in the study below;
  • to repeat the computation of a release before 0.2.1, so that a fit made with such a release can be reproduced exactly. This also requires the same Stan version, compiler, and seed.

What predictor reuse changes

The comparator evidence in a survival fit is reconstructed pseudo-individual data. The likelihood of each pseudo-individual is integrated over the covariate distribution of the comparator population, as in the population adjustment of multilevel network meta-regression (Phillippo et al. 2020). With integration points \(x_{a1}, \dots, x_{aM}\) for comparator arm \(a\), the likelihood contribution of pseudo-individual \(j\), with observed time \(t_j\) and event indicator \(d_j\), is

\[ L_j = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^{M} f\!\left(t_j, d_j \mid \eta_{am}\right), \qquad \eta_{am} = \mu_{C} + x_{am}^{\top} \beta, \]

where \(f\) is the density or survival function of the chosen distribution, \(\mu_C\) is the comparator intercept, and \(\beta\) is the vector of comparator covariate coefficients.

The linear predictors \(\eta_{a1}, \dots, \eta_{aM}\) depend on the arm and the parameters but not on \(j\). Releases before 0.2.1 nevertheless recomputed them for every pseudo-individual: one product of the \(M \times n_B\) design matrix with the coefficient vector per pseudo-individual and per evaluation of the log density, where \(n_B\) is the number of design columns. With predictor reuse they are computed once per arm and evaluation, and shared by all pseudo-individuals in the arm.

Stan computes the gradient of the log density by reverse-mode automatic differentiation (Carpenter et al. 2017), and the No-U-Turn sampler (Hoffman and Gelman 2014) evaluates this gradient at every leapfrog step. Each matrix-vector product creates \(M\) new values in the expression graph, with the bookkeeping needed to differentiate them, and in the reverse pass propagates \(M\) adjoints into \(n_B\) coefficients. Predictor reuse removes all but one product per arm. It leaves unchanged the \(n_{\text{agd}} \times M\) likelihood terms \(f\), the likelihood of the index study, and the priors.

The saving per evaluation is therefore the share of the total cost that the removed products account for. It should be larger when the design is wider (more covariates, or the relaxed model with its separate comparator coefficients) and the likelihood terms are inexpensive. It should be smaller when the likelihood terms or the index study dominate the cost.

Same posterior, different final digits

Both computations evaluate the same function. The matrix-vector products give identical values, but the reverse pass adds up the contributions to each coefficient’s gradient in a different order: once per arm after summing over pseudo-individuals, instead of once per pseudo-individual. Floating-point addition is not associative, so the two gradients can differ in their final digits.

The posterior distribution is the same. Under a common seed the draws coincide until such differences accumulate along the sampler’s trajectory, which can happen as early as warmup, and they agree in distribution throughout. The package’s parity tests (tests/parity/ in the source repository) compare, for each of 52 test cases and both settings, the log density, its gradient, the parameter transforms, and every generated quantity against the 0.2.0 models.

Simulation design

The study answers three questions:

  1. How much time does predictor reuse save per evaluation of the log density and its gradient, the operation repeated at every leapfrog step, and how does the saving depend on the distribution, the model, the number of comparator pseudo-individuals \(n_{\text{agd}}\), the number of integration points \(M\), and the number of covariates \(K\)?
  2. How much of that saving reaches a complete mlumr() fit?
  3. Did the computations with and without predictor reuse, and the 0.2.0 models, give the same values in every run?

The questions concern computation time and numerical agreement, not bias or coverage. The script that ran the study is a development file, kept in the package repository and not installed with the package: data-raw/simulate_survival_optimization.R. It writes a manifest beside its results recording the software and hardware used. The numbers below were read from results files with MD5 digests 4dfebf427fcd54c36ecf9239b7db15d9 (per-evaluation timings) and b61eca06deeeb989d24083986760313e (complete fits); a package test compares these digests against the files in the repository.

The data

One data-generating mechanism is used throughout, as a benchmark. Computation time depends mainly on the code path of the fitted model and on the sample sizes. It also depends on the data values: through the mix of event and censoring indicators, through branches of the generalized gamma likelihood that the values of time and of the parameters select, and, in complete fits, through the sampler’s trajectories. The study does not vary these systematically.

  • An index study of 200 individuals and a comparator cohort of \(n_{\text{agd}}\) individuals are generated in the same way.
  • The \(K\) covariates alternate between continuous (normal, SD 1) and binary. The comparator population is shifted by 0.2 on the continuous covariates and has prevalence 0.45, against 0.5 in the index population, on the binary covariates.
  • Event times follow a Weibull proportional hazards model with shape 1.2, coefficients 0.3, -0.2, 0.15, -0.1, 0.25, and -0.15 (the first \(K\) of these), a baseline median of about five months, and a conditional log hazard ratio of -0.4 for the index treatment.
  • Censoring times are independent and exponential with rate 0.02 per month, with administrative censoring at 24 months: right censoring only, without delayed entry.
  • The comparator cohort’s times and event indicators are supplied to set_agd_surv() as its pseudo-individual data, with its realized covariate means, standard deviations, and proportions. For computation this is equivalent to a digitized Kaplan-Meier curve.
  • Integration points come from add_integration() with its default Gaussian copula. The restricted mean survival time (RMST) is computed to 18 months, and predictions are made at 6, 12, and 18 months.

Each data set is shared by every distribution and model of the same size, so those comparisons are made on the same individuals.

Three computations

  • With predictor reuse (optimization = TRUE): the 0.2.1 survival models, which compute the linear predictor once per comparator arm.
  • Without predictor reuse (optimization = FALSE): the same compiled models, which recompute the linear predictor for every pseudo-individual.
  • Version 0.2.0: the survival models as released in mlumr 0.2.0, kept unchanged in the repository for the parity tests. They answer two further questions: whether 0.2.1 is faster than the previous release, and whether the switch between the two computations adds any cost when predictor reuse is off.

Level A: one evaluation at a time

Workloads. Every combination of distribution (Weibull, log-normal, generalized gamma, and the M-spline flexible baseline), model (SPFA and relaxed), \(n_{\text{agd}}\) (100, 500, 2000), \(M\) (16, 64, 256), and \(K\) (1, 3, 6): 216 workloads, each with two data sets. The piecewise exponential baseline shares the likelihood code of the M-spline baseline and is not measured separately.

Measurement. Time is measured inside the CmdStan executable that mlumr(engine = "cmdstanr") uses, through CmdStan’s log_prob method. It reads a file of parameter values and, for each row, evaluates the log density and its gradient and writes the result. The CPU time of a run over \(N\) rows, minus the median CPU time of three runs over five rows, divided by \(N - 5\), estimates the cost per row without process start-up, data input, and model construction. The three short runs are made once per data set and method, so all blocks of a data set share this subtraction.

The cost per row is one evaluation of the log density and its gradient, plus reading one row of parameter values and writing one row of results. The reading and writing are the same for the three computations; they dilute the relative savings slightly for the least expensive workloads without favoring any of them. \(N\) is chosen per data set so that a run lasts about 0.5 s, with a minimum of 20 rows, and is the same for all three computations. The minimum applied to 17% of runs, and the longest run took 5.2 s of CPU time.

Parameter values. The timings use five draws from CmdStan’s Laplace approximation at the posterior mode of each data set, so that they are taken where the sampler spends its time. For 67 of the 432 data sets (57 generalized gamma and 10 Weibull) the posterior mode could not be located, and the origin together with four small perturbations of it was used instead.

Pairing. Each data set is timed in twelve blocks, and each block runs each of the three computations once. The blocks use each of the six possible orders of the three computations two times, in random sequence, so every computation runs in every position in four of the twelve blocks of every data set. Data sets are processed in random order, so that any drift in timing over the run is spread across the experimental factors.

Estimate and interval. For each block the quantity of interest is the log ratio of the time per evaluation without predictor reuse to the time with it, and likewise for the 0.2.0 models. The blocks of a data set share the subtracted cost of the short runs, so the data set, not the block, is the independent unit. The estimate for a workload is the mean of its two data-set means, each the mean of twelve block log ratios.

With two data sets per workload, the variance between data-set means cannot be estimated reliably for each workload on its own. It is pooled over the 18 workloads that share the distribution and the number of integration points. The two models of a given size are fitted to the same data sets, so these workloads hold 9 independent pairs of data sets, and the 95% interval for a workload uses the \(t\) distribution with 9 degrees of freedom. Results are reported as the percentage saving in time per evaluation, \(100\,(1 - 1/\text{ratio})\).

Level B: complete fits

Scenarios. Two SPFA scenarios with three covariates and 64 integration points: a Weibull baseline with 500 comparator pseudo-individuals and an M-spline baseline with 300, each with ten data sets.

Fits. Each data set is fitted with optimization = TRUE and with optimization = FALSE through mlumr(engine = "cmdstanr") with the default sampler settings (four chains of 2000 iterations, 1000 of them warmup, adapt_delta = 0.95, max_treedepth = 15). The four chains run in parallel, and both fits use the same seed and hence the same initial values. The order of the two settings alternates between data sets, so each order is used for half of the data sets. Draws are written with 18 significant digits.

A chain follows a common trajectory under both settings when, at every sampling iteration, each sampled parameter (the regression coefficients and the auxiliary and spline parameters) differs between the settings by at most \(10^{-6} \max(1, |x|)\), where \(x\) is its value with predictor reuse. This tolerance admits the rounding differences described above but not a separation of trajectories.

What is compared. The main quantity is the sampling time per leapfrog step, paired by data set and chain. It is dominated by the cost of a step within the actual sampler. The sampling time also includes work done once per iteration (the generated quantities and the writing of draws), so it is not entirely independent of the number of steps per iteration, but it is much less sensitive to that number than the elapsed time is. The elapsed time of the mlumr() call and the bulk effective sample size (ESS) of the comparator-population RMST difference per second of elapsed time are also reported. Each is summarized as the mean of the log ratios over data sets, with a \(t\) interval; with ten data sets these intervals are wide.

What the study found

In short: predictor reuse cut the time per evaluation of the log density and its gradient by 12.6% in the median workload and by up to 28%. The largest savings came with the M-spline baseline, wide designs, and few integration points, and the smallest with the generalized gamma distribution. In complete fits the elapsed time fell by about 10% with the Weibull baseline and 23% with the M-spline baseline. Because the optimization is on by default, survival fits get this saving without any change to user code.

The computations agree to the final digits

Across the 432 data sets of Level A, the largest absolute difference in the log density or in any component of its gradient between the computations with and without predictor reuse, over all parameter values timed, was 1.6e-10; between predictor reuse and the 0.2.0 models it was 1.6e-10. In every data set the two differences were identical, which is consistent with the computation without predictor reuse and the 0.2.0 models producing identical output.

The saving was largest for the M-spline baseline (18.9% on average over the other factors) and smallest for the generalized gamma distribution (5.1%), whose likelihood terms evaluate incomplete gamma functions and so leave the removed products a smaller share of the total cost.

The other factors behave as the structure of the computation predicts. The saving grew with the number of covariates, from 11.2% with one covariate to 14.4% with six, and was larger for the relaxed model than for the SPFA model (13.4% against 12.0%), since both widen the design. It grew slightly with the number of pseudo-individuals, from 11.3% at 100 to 13.5% at 2000, as the fixed cost of the index study’s likelihood became a smaller share of the total.

It fell with the number of integration points, from 16.2% at 16 to 11.2% at 64 and 10.4% at 256. The operation count alone does not predict this, because both the removed products and the likelihood terms grow linearly with \(M\). A fall followed by a plateau is consistent with a cost per product that does not grow with \(M\), such as the bookkeeping needed to differentiate each product, which is a larger share of the total when \(M\) is small.

Turning reuse off costs what 0.2.0 cost

The savings relative to the 0.2.0 models are close to those relative to the computation without predictor reuse, because the two take nearly the same time. Averaged over workloads, the 0.2.0 models took 0.5% longer than the computation without predictor reuse (95% interval 0.2% to 0.8%, computed over the 27 data-set sizes). The data flag that selects between the two computations does not slow down optimization = FALSE.

In the Weibull scenario predictor reuse cut the sampling time per leapfrog step by 11.8% (95% interval 9.1% to 14.5%), in line with the Level A estimate of 8.4% (95% interval 5.1% to 11.5%) for the same distribution, model, sample size, number of integration points, and number of covariates. The elapsed time of the mlumr() call fell by 9.6% (95% interval 8.0% to 11.3%), and the bulk ESS of the RMST difference per second rose by a factor of 1.11 (95% interval 1.08 to 1.14).

In the M-spline scenario the point estimates were larger: a saving of 20.7% (95% interval 15.3% to 25.8%) per leapfrog step and 23.3% (95% interval 14.0% to 31.7%) in elapsed time, against 15.3% (95% interval 13.2% to 17.3%) per evaluation in Level A at 500 pseudo-individuals. Both intervals exclude no change. In the Weibull scenario the saving in elapsed time is smaller than the saving per leapfrog step, as expected when work that predictor reuse does not touch dilutes it. In the M-spline scenario it is larger. No M-spline chain followed a common trajectory under the two settings, so the number of leapfrog steps differed between them by chance; the fits without predictor reuse took 2.2% more leapfrog steps in sampling in total. These differences reflect the sampler’s paths rather than an effect of predictor reuse. That is why the time per leapfrog step is the main quantity compared.

Checks on the timing design

Position within a block. Times were slightly shorter in the first position of a block: relative to it, times were on average 0.0% longer in the second position and 0.1% longer in the third. A linear model for the log time per workload, with effects of block, computation, and position, gives estimates adjusted for position. The adjustment changed the workload estimates of the saving by a median of 0.0 and at most 0.0 percentage points, and changed the mean saving over all workloads from 12.7% to 12.7%.

Fallback parameter values. In the 39 workloads in which one data set was timed at the fallback parameter values and the other at Laplace draws, the log time ratio at the fallback values minus that at the Laplace draws, averaged within each of the 23 data-set sizes involved and then over sizes, was 0.004 (95% interval -0.008 to 0.015), with standard deviation 0.032 across workloads. For comparison, the difference between the two data sets of the workloads in which both used Laplace draws had standard deviation 0.023.

What this vignette does not claim

The timings come from one machine and one compiler, through CmdStan (engine = "cmdstanr") only. Absolute times, and to a lesser extent the relative savings, depend on the processor, the compiler, and its optimization flags. The results are not claimed to hold on other hardware or for the rstan engine.

The two levels measure different things. Level A is the CPU time of a single process evaluating the log density and its gradient at fixed parameter values. Level B is the elapsed time of complete mlumr() calls whose four chains ran in parallel, which also includes data preparation, the generated quantities, reading the sampler output, and post-processing, and depends on how many leapfrog steps the sampler takes. When both settings take the same number of steps, the work that predictor reuse does not touch dilutes the saving, so the saving in elapsed time should be smaller than the saving per evaluation. Once trajectories separate, the number of steps differs between the settings by chance, and the saving in elapsed time of a single fit can be larger or smaller than the saving per evaluation, as in the M-spline scenario.

The Level A intervals assume that the variance between the data-set means of a workload is the same for all workloads with the same distribution and number of integration points. With two data sets per workload this cannot be checked for each workload, and the pooled estimates for workloads that share data sets are correlated. The pooled variance includes both the variation between data sets and the error of the short runs subtracted from all blocks of a data set.

Level B has two scenarios with ten data sets each, and its intervals are wide. The saving in elapsed time for other distributions, sample sizes, numbers of integration points, or for the relaxed model was not measured directly; it can be inferred only from the corresponding Level A workloads.

One data-generating mechanism was used, with right censoring only. Delayed entry and left or interval censoring add terms to each likelihood evaluation and would be expected to shrink the share of the cost that predictor reuse removes. For 67 data sets the timings were taken at fallback parameter values rather than at draws near the posterior mode. The cost of the incomplete gamma functions in the generalized gamma likelihood depends on the parameter values, but within workloads the savings at the two kinds of points did not differ systematically (see the checks above). That comparison is between different data sets; no data set was timed at both kinds of points.

Each unit started only when the load average was below the stated threshold, but the machine was not otherwise idle; the recorded load averages are reported above. Background activity adds variability to single timings. In Level A, the random order of data sets and the placement of every computation in every position within blocks limit its effect on the comparison. In Level B, the order of the two fits of a pair may also affect timing; each order was used for half of the data sets of each scenario.

The processor has four performance cores and four efficiency cores, and the operating system decided which processes ran on which. A chain scheduled on an efficiency core runs more slowly, which is a plausible source of the variability between data sets in Level B, where four chains ran at once.

Reproducing the study

From the root of the package source, with that source installed:

# Each stage records every completed unit and resumes from there if interrupted.
system("Rscript data-raw/simulate_survival_optimization.R fixed")
system("Rscript data-raw/simulate_survival_optimization.R fits")
system("Rscript data-raw/simulate_survival_optimization.R assemble")

The final stage writes the results files and the manifest, which records the commit of the package source, the software versions, and the machine.
